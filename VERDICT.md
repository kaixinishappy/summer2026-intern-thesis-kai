# FinTech Disruption of Traditional Banking

**Thesis question:** Digital payment platforms, neobanks, and embedded finance players have taken significant market share from legacy institutions. Thesis evaluate whether FinTech disruption has run its course or whether the second wave of AI-native financial services is just beginning

## Verdict

The **first wave of fintech has effectively ended.** Between 2010 and 2021, payment platforms, neobanks, and challengers like PayPal, Block, and Robinhood won on user experience, not a fundamentally different banking model. Many turned unprofitable after 2021 while rising rates strengthened bank earnings, and traditional institutions have since reclaimed the pricing power and market position fintech briefly took. This is backed by price and profitability data across multiple years, and by three converging robustness checks: fintech's share of the combined revenue pool stayed essentially flat (~19% → 20%, 2022-2025) even as the challengers returned to profit — a direct market-share read, not just a stock-price proxy; the fintech basket underperformed even the broad Nasdaq-100 growth benchmark (QQQ), so the decline is fintech-specific rather than the 2022 growth-stock selloff; and a fintech-sector-ETF-vs-bank-sector-ETF check shows the same rise-and-rollover shape beyond the 7 tickers picked here.

The **second wave, AI-native finance**, can't be evaluated with the same confidence. Most AI-first firms are private or too newly public for meaningful data, so there's no way yet to tell lasting advantage from early hype. Search interest and AI-related filings show only modest upticks starting 2024. That thinness is itself the finding: too early to call, not absent.

The second wave is where the two camps split — and *reading* the 2026 filings rather than counting them shows how. **The fintechs are the ones experimenting with AI to win customers.** PayPal and Block are the only names in the sample whose filings describe actually *deploying* AI agents, and PayPal frames it plainly as a growth play — giving consumers "more reasons to use PayPal and Venmo." **The big banks take the opposite posture: they treat agentic AI as a large risk to be governed, not a product to rush out.** JPMorgan, HSBC and Barclays name it almost entirely as a threat — disintermediation "if AI agents autonomously manage financial decisions," cyber exposure, "liability, reputational harm, regulatory actions" — with HSBC applying "We have enhanced our inventory control to apply heightened scrutiny of agentic AI use cases before deployment" and building governance "to manage the range of risks" the technology introduces.

That split reframes the disruption question. If the banks' risk-and-governance stance is not slowness but a **deliberate strategy** — absorb AI on their own terms, under control, and make sure it is used correctly — then the fintechs' AI push need not repeat Wave 1. The prediction this project leans toward, and labels clearly as a prediction rather than a finding, is that **banks can absorb the AI wave the way they absorbed the first**: they have the profits to fund it and the stronger incentive to get the governance right, and if they do, an AI-native challenger may never get the room to disrupt them at scale. This rests on one LLM's read of 19 filing passages, not a hard statistic — and the thing that would break it is exactly the private, AI-native entrant a public-company sample cannot see.

The project's main empirical contribution is separating these two waves rather than treating fintech disruption as one continuous story. Wave 1 and Wave 2 run on different dynamics and different timelines, and blending them risks misattributing what's actually happening.


## Bottom line

**Fintech didn't die, and AI hasn't clearly disrupted anything yet.** By the measures available here (price, profitability, search attention), Wave 1 has effectively ended — substantially reabsorbed by the banks it once threatened, most visible in the 2022 net-income split where challengers posted losses the same year bank profits grew, and confirmed at the broader sector level (FINX vs. KBWB) so it isn't just the 7 tickers picked. The second wave is early and **split by posture**: the fintechs (PayPal, Block) are starting to deploy AI and AI agents to attract customers, while the banks are treating AI as a large risk to be governed rather than a product to rush out. The bet this project leans toward — not something it can prove — is that this governance-first stance is the banks' *path to absorbing* the AI wave, not evidence they are losing it: if they can adopt AI under proper governance and make sure it is used correctly, fintech's AI advantage may never translate into the disruption Wave 1 once threatened. The honest caveat stays attached: this is a prediction resting on one LLM's read of 19 filing passages, and a private, AI-native challenger — the kind this public-company sample cannot see — is exactly what would break it.
